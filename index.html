while it's still the frontier.

Cultigen is an AI platform that helps educators — from K-12 to universities — keep their curricula on the frontier: diagnosing what to teach, coaching faculty to teach it, and turning frontier research — or a real-world industry process or product — into lessons, interactives, and buildable kits.

Curricula lag for two reasons —
and neither is a lack of ideas.

The first is cost. Reviewing, revising, and re-approving courses across departments takes committees, semesters, and budgets — so a program only refreshes every few years. The constraint was never authorship; it was amortization.

The second is craft. Designing a good course — its pacing, scaffolding, and assessment — is a discipline in its own right, yet most faculty were never trained in it. For generations it's been tacit, experiential know-how, picked up on the job. Even a professor eager to modernize often lacks the method and the time to do it well.

So what students learn lags the frontier by years — from a middle-school science class to a graduate seminar — and drifts out of step with what the workforce needs. And when a course finally does modernize, the hands-on kits that make it stick are expensive and hard to customize. Cultigen attacks both: it guides faculty through course design and accelerates every step.

FunguyLab: proof that
the kits can be built.

FunguyLab is a bioprinter that uses light and AI to guide living fungi into designed forms — a hands-on kit students can build in class, learning across biology, engineering, and design at once.

It grew out of our research, published at SIGGRAPH — the top computer-graphics venue. It began as an early proof of concept, and is now being adopted by universities and community labs. It's the clearest evidence that Cultigen's hands-on kits aren't a slide — they ship.

Course quality is a bigger
problem than it looks.

When teaching drifts, the cost lands on real people — students, graduates, and the public that funds research.

Is a degree still worth it?

In the AI age, more and more people question the value of higher education. The answer isn't a smaller university — it's making what happens inside one undeniably good.

Big debt, uneven teaching

Students take on serious debt for degrees whose teaching quality still has enormous room to improve. If the price is real, the learning should be too.

Taught vs. actually needed

What schools teach and what the world needs have drifted apart. Graduates leave underprepared and talent goes to waste — while employers still can't fill the roles they have. A curriculum in step with practice closes both gaps at once.

Research stuck at the frontier

The path from a new discovery to a teachable lesson is slow and costly, so students keep learning yesterday's material. Better tools shorten that path.

Knowledge that never arrives

Too much research stops at "published." Work that could reach and benefit people never becomes something students can learn from and build on.

None of this means schools are obsolete.

It means the quality of the course is the thing worth fixing — and the thing Cultigen is built to fix.

Why I'm building Cultigen.

I've taken courses at top schools on both sides of the Pacific — Brown, RISD, Harvard and MIT in the US, and Tsinghua University and the China Academy of Art in China. Between them they span engineering universities, comprehensive Ivy-League schools, and world-leading art-and-design institutes, across two countries.

And even at these elite institutions, course quality was wildly uneven. Too many classes were outdated and dull, and did little to help students solve real problems in their field. In one course I realized the professor's slides were ten years old — the material had gone stale long ago. Others held genuinely valuable material, but taught it so poorly that the conversion from what a course covers to what a student actually learns was heartbreakingly low.

But one course has stayed with me ever since — a neural-engineering class at Harvard. I arrived with zero background in the field. In a single semester I walked away with a whole toolkit of genuinely usable skills: the theory, hands-on lab work, connections to hospital resources, direct contact with frontier research, the confidence to frame original research questions, and even how to write a grant proposal.

That is what a course can be. My goal with Cultigen is to bring that kind of extraordinary experience to every university — and lift teaching quality across the board.
